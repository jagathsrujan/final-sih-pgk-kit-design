The problem · the deliverable set

Four subsystems, one fuze-sized volume

  • Canard actuation. A lateral force on an airframe spinning at 1,108–1,912 rad/s.
  • Guidance, navigation and control. Predict the miss, command a correction, at 1 kHz inside the loop.
  • Multi-mode fuze. Proximity, time and impact, each with its own safety logic.
  • Power and interface. Managed through a 15,000 g launch; programmed at the gun before firing.

Our design · the architecture

Two bodies, one interface

  • The ring. Four fixed canards, free to rotate about the axis, driven by the machine inside it.
  • The machine. A brushless machine between ring and body: it turns the ring, and it generates.
  • The stack. Computer, sensors and fuze in the well behind the machine.

The ring angle is the only command channel. There is no actuator, no linkage and no flight battery.

Two bodies in relative rotation, and the interface between them is the whole design: the force goes one way, the power comes back the other.

Our design · where the force comes from

The force is a cant angle, not an actuator

  • Each fin makes one aerodynamic force, normal to its own surface.
  • Mount it at a cant angle and that force has a sideways component that steers the shell.
  • The cant sets the direction; the ring's angle decides which way it points.
≥ 18.1 N

Our design · the fin arrangement

One pair steers, the other turns the ring

The ring as machined: four fixed canards in two pairs.

  • The steering pair is set against the body's cant, and puts the side force on the shell.
  • The rotation pair is set against the other, and puts a torque on the ring — that torque is how the ring is aimed and held.

This split is published prior work for this airframe class; we cite it and build the powered, computed version of it.

The elegance of it: the ring is aimed by the same aerodynamic force that steers the shell, so the control authority and the disturbance are the same quantity.

Holding an angle costs torque, and the reaction to that torque is what turns the generator. So the control budget and the power budget are the same number, spent twice.

No flight battery exists because this machine is always turning: the power is a by-product of holding the ring.

Our design · stability

The risk is not the muzzle margin, it is resonance

  • Canards sit ahead of the centre of gravity, so their force pitches the nose up — a destabilising moment.
  • The worse case is not that margin: the fin disturbance can resonate with the airframe's own pitching motion, and grow.
  • The fix is the one this literature recommends: small span, and take the force from cant rather than area.

A muzzle stability factor does not answer this, because resonance is an angular-motion effect rather than a gyroscopic margin.

Guidance · the law

Predict where it lands, then ask for a direction

Every cycle, the law propagates the round's own states to impact, compares that point with the target, and commands the ring angle that reduces the difference. It is a continuous prediction, not a scheduled profile.

  • One command channel: the ring angle
  • Re-solved continuously, so late errors matter more than early ones
  • The predicted point moves as navigation improves — that is the mechanism, not a fault

Continuous impact-point prediction: the miss we command against is always the miss the round would have had if we stopped steering now.

Guidance · the constraint that shapes it

The ring gives a direction, not an impulse

The fins are always making force. All the ring can do is choose which way it points — there is no position where the force is off.

  • So the command is aimed, never merely applied
  • A wrong command does not cancel out; it has to be reversed
  • The ring must also be held against the aerodynamic torque, or the force re-points itself

This is what makes the guidance law different from a fin-controlled missile: the disturbance and the control are the same force, and we only get to rotate it.

Our contribution · the novelty claim

Command it only when it is worth commanding

Every cycle, before any command leaves the computer, the gate asks two questions and compares the answers.

  • What would a useful correction need? From the predicted miss, and the time and authority left to remove it.
  • What can the machine deliver right now? From the measured relative speed and the machine's own capability.

Commanded

  • the correction is larger than what the loop can already remove
  • the machine can hold it for the time it takes
  • the ring is clear of the friction gate

Withheld

  • nothing useful is available — the round coasts rather than wastes authority
  • the command would be noise the loop then has to undo
  • the ring cannot be held, so the force would re-point itself

This gate, and a machine that is both the actuator and the generator, is the entire novelty claim — the rest we took from the published record on purpose.

Our contribution · the gate16 / 20

Control · the loops

One fast loop on the ring, one careful loop on the trajectory

The ring is the only thing that has to move quickly; the trajectory loop has seconds to think, and it uses them.

  • Inner: ring rate, at 1 kHz — it holds the angle the outer loop asks for
  • Outer: guidance and attitude, slow and bounded by what the ring can do
  • Observer: estimates what the body is doing, so the loops do not fight the spin

Splitting the two is what makes a 1 kHz loop affordable on a flight processor running the fuze and navigation as well.

Decisions · before 30 September

Three things I want from this review

  1. The novelty claim. Two pairs of fixed canards on a de-spun correction part are published. Ours is the gate, and the machine that is actuator and generator. Is that the claim you would defend, or is there a better one in this design?
  2. The evidence gap. We have no range time, so 30 m rests on a budget of 23.9 m and on published flight data. Should the time after selection go into simulation depth, or into a hardware-in-the-loop rig?
  3. Where the six slides spend their space. Five prescribed sections, and a register of 196 criteria behind them. Which of these twenty slides would you put in front of an evaluator?

The six slides are what is judged on 30 September; everything in the briefing behind them is what those slides are answerable to.
