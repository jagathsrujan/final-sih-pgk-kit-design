DRISHTI-PGK: a precision guidance kit and multi-mode fuze for the 155 mm shell Technical briefing — design basis, hardware, flight behaviour and control architecture

The kit replaces the fuze and the forward ogive of a standard 155 mm high-explosive shell. Four fixed fins on a de-spun ring steer the round; the machine that turns the ring also generates the kit's electrical power. There are no actuators, no linkage and no flight battery. The shell, the charge system and the gun are untouched.

This briefing covers the physics the design rests on, every part and what it does, the flight from launch to impact, the guidance and control stack, and the state of the evidence: what the CAD has verified, what is still open, and where the design rests on a published figure rather than our own measurement.

Every quantity on these pages traces to a row in the design criteria register. Page references for each figure and table are given in the captions; the register is the authority if anything here disagrees with it.

Architecture: two bodies in relative rotation, and one interface between them

The kit screws into the existing fuze well, so the shell, the charge system and the gun stay as they are. Inside the kit there are only two bodies: everything that turns with the shell, and the ring, which does not. The bearing pair carries the structural load between them; the electric machine carries the torque.

Why this architecture

  • Steering comes from the cant of the fins, not from moving surfaces, so aiming the force means turning one ring. Two-dimensional steering needs one actuation axis.
  • The same machine that turns the ring generates the kit's power, so there is no flight battery to charge, store or qualify for shelf life.
  • The fins are permanently exposed. There is no deployment mechanism and no stored spring, so nothing can deploy in the bore — the in-bore question becomes a clearance calculation rather than a mechanism.
  • The fuze keeps its own processor, board and power path. The guidance computer can send a bounded one-way time correction and a veto, and nothing else.

One aerodynamic force per fin, and the cant decides which way it points

Each fin is a flat plate fixed to the ring and twisted by a small angle to the airflow. That twist is the cant. The resulting force has a component that translates the shell and a component that turns the ring, and the designer chooses how the two are shared by choosing the cant.

Why the force cannot be commanded

The fins sit in the airflow from the moment the round leaves the muzzle. Their force scales with dynamic pressure and with cant angle, and both are fixed by then. Nothing in the guidance system can switch the force off or reduce it; the law can only choose the direction it points in and how long it stays there. That single constraint shapes the rest of the design, from the guidance window to the duty cycle on page 26.

The span cap tightens it further. Forward of the ogive the shell is only about 98 mm across, so the fins cannot be made wider. Chord is a poor substitute: going from a 30 mm to an 80 mm chord is 2.7 times the area for about 23 % more force, because the aspect ratio collapses. Cant angle remains the useful lever, and cant also raises the ring torque and the energy that has to be dissipated, as page 8 shows.

The 18.1 N requirement does not change, but the coupling between cant, ring torque and dumped power depends on it, so the row is flagged for correction rather than quietly edited.

Four fins in two pairs: one steers the shell, the other turns the ring

The fins are mounted at 0°, 90°, 180° and 270°. The pair on one axis is canted the same way on both fins; the pair on the other axis is canted oppositely. That single choice separates the two jobs: one pair produces a net force on the shell, the other produces a net torque on the ring.

The control pair is not optional

The electric machine is a generator. Its reaction torque acts in one sense only, so on its own it can brake the ring's rotation but never drive it the other way. Something else has to produce torque in the opposite sense, and in this design that something is aerodynamic: the control pair. Without it, the ring cannot be held against the aerodynamic torque and the whole control scheme fails.

That is why the control pair appears in the sizing arithmetic on the next page rather than in a list of options. The aerodynamic torque it generates is a designed quantity, and it is squeezed from both sides: it has to exceed the bearing friction, and it has to stay inside what the machine can hold against.

Reading the arrangement

Figure 3(b) on the previous page shows the arrangement as seen down the axis. Because the ring can be turned to any azimuth and held, the guidance force can be pointed anywhere in the plane perpendicular to the shell's axis. Steering is therefore two-dimensional even though there is only one actuation axis: the ring angle is the command, and the force follows it.

The magnitude of that force is not commanded, and this has a consequence that runs through the whole flight. The fins push from launch to impact, so the trajectory is continuously perturbed whether or not a correction is wanted. The guidance law can null the average effect over a cycle by time-sharing the aim direction, but it can never stop the instantaneous push. The propagation model in the guidance law has to treat the steering force as a permanent term, not as an event — this is the largest single error term in the accuracy budget on page 31.

The ring is a balance of three torques, and friction sets whether it closes

Holding the ring at a commanded angle means winning a tug-of-war that lasts the whole flight. Aerodynamic torque pulls one way; the machine and the bearings push the other. The arithmetic of that balance is the tightest constraint in the kit, and one of the three terms is a bearing property we have to specify.

The two criteria, evaluated in flight rather than at a design point

Write the control pair's size as a scale factor k on the reference aerodynamic torque. At apogee the controllability criterion sets a floor under k; on the descent the symmetric criterion sets a ceiling on it. The two bounds meet at a friction torque of 0.1567 N·m. Scaling the reference's own viscous coefficient to that speed predicts 1.7 N·m at apogee — ten times the aerodynamic torque, and a failure of the whole balance. The machine is both the actuator and the generator

A frameless outrunner is built into the joint between the ring and the body: magnets on the ring, coils on the body, so the windings see the shell's full spin. Drawing current produces a reaction torque on the ring; not drawing it produces electricity. Holding the ring does both at once, and that is where the kit's thermal load comes from.

Torque, and why the bus voltage is a free choice

T = 3 · p · ψPM · I_rms / √2 at fixed slot area: T = p · k_w · Φpole · fill · A_slot · J / √2

The turns count cancels. At a fixed slot area the torque depends on pole count, flux per pole, slot area and current density, and not on how the winding is split between volts and amps. Copper loss is therefore the same at any bus voltage, and the voltage can be chosen on other grounds. We chose it on partial-discharge risk at altitude, not on efficiency: the reference machine's constants imply a 1.4 kV machine and a 1.4 kV rectifier, which is a real risk in a potted assembly for no thermal gain.

Holding the ring costs kilowatts, and all of it leaves as heat

The machine holds the ring against the shell's own rotation, so the mechanical power it absorbs is the aerodynamic torque multiplied by the spin rate. At launch that product is 2.92 kW, and over the flight it integrates to about 121 kJ. This is the price of the de-spun architecture, and it is the number we put in front of a reviewer ourselves.

A second heat load that is easy to miss

The bearing budget is also a heat budget. A friction torque of 0.10 N·m at a relative speed of 1,400–1,900 rad/s is 140–190 W, continuous, dissipated inside the bearings rather than in the dump. It is roughly a tenth of the machine's total, and it means the preload that buys bearing stiffness has to be justified thermally as well as structurally.

How the energy leaves

A chopper-driven shunt regulator on the bus burns the surplus: an element of 0.15–0.30 kg, bonded to the stator mount, sharing the conduction path into the shell through the seat and the well bore. During launch the kit's own metal (400–450 J/K) buffers the transient — about a 40 K rise — and the interface is sized on the 90 s average rather than the peak.

Fins ahead of the centre of gravity are the real stability risk

A 155 mm shell is only marginally stable and stays stable by spinning. Anything that adds a lifting surface ahead of the centre of gravity works against that, so the canards are the one component that could veto the architecture. This page is how we bound them.

The whole margin at the envelope edge is 5 % of the pitching-moment derivative, so the canard set has to be sized against stability and not only against force. Two results make that less uncomfortable than it looks. The ring and magnets add 5.4 % to the axial inertia, which raises the stability factor by about 11 % and partly pays for the fins' own effect. And stability at the muzzle does not depend on which charge is loaded: spin is set by the rifling, so a light charge and a heavy charge leave the tube equally stable, and the standard 1 turn in 20 calibres reproduces the published charge table at both ends.

We do not use the transverse inertia carried in the reference work. It is about 28 % below the value two independent public routes give, and since Sg rises when Iyy falls, using it would flatter the one result that can veto the design. The conservative band is used instead.

A failure mode with its own name

Gyroscopic stability is not the whole story. The literature on fixed-canard dual-spin projectiles documents a roll–coning resonance in which the disturbance from the fins resonates with the shell's natural pitching frequency and the motion grows. A muzzle stability factor does not answer it, because it is an angular-motion effect rather than a gyroscopic margin.

The design has already taken the fix that the same literature recommends: reduce the span and get the force from cant rather than from area, which raises the natural frequency clear of the disturbance. Our span cap of 151 mm and the decision to buy force with cant angle are that recipe. What is owed is the evidence, and it is scheduled with the rest of the flight analysis.

The asymmetry that matters

Correcting range and correcting deflection are not interchangeable. A deflection correction is close to a pure sideways displacement and can be spent late in the flight; a range correction has to fight the velocity vector, changes the time of flight and costs more authority for the same metres. Our dispersion anchor is published as a single circular figure and does not split into the two directions, so the budget is carried as an isotropic case until the customer's firing tables arrive.

Where the effort should go

The endgame is not about force. With 10 s to go, 3 m/s of leftover lateral velocity is 30 m of impact error; with 30 s to go, about 1 m/s is. Nulling the velocity well enough matters more than adding canard area, and the error budget on page 31 reaches the same conclusion from the other direction: guidance authority closes, while navigation quality and prediction accuracy are what the 30 m claim actually rests on.

Sensing on a body that spins 260 times a second

The hardest engineering problem in this class of shell is not the guidance law. It is measuring anything at all while the airframe rotates at up to 304 revolutions per second. Three of the four sensing decisions exist because of that one fact.

Table 22 — The three decisions the spin forces, and what they cost
ProblemAnswer
A body-fixed antenna would have its pattern swept 260 times a secondthe aperture sits on the spin axis, rotationally symmetric, with its elements combined rather than switched. The spin then produces no modulation by symmetry, and a standard receiver suffices.
No commercial inertial sensor survives the roll ratethe unit rides the de-spun ring; a body-fixed unit would have to resolve about 92,880°/s on the roll axis, where the commercial ceiling is 2,000–4,000°/s.
The ground return is modulated by the body's rotationthe proximity detector rejects that modulation explicitly, or it bursts at the wrong height.

The ring mount costs power and data across a rotating joint, which page 15 covers: a rotary transformer for power and an optical channel for data.

The magnetometer, stated plainly

The earth-referenced ring angle is what aims the guidance force, and it comes from a magnetometer fused into the navigation filter. Its interference is the machine: the magnets pass a body-fixed point at 1,234–2,131 Hz, and the coil current makes a field that tracks the flight phase.

Shielding does not solve this. The interference and the measurement occupy the same band and the same space, so a shield that removes one removes the other. The answer is spectral and temporal instead — a coherent notch locked to the measured relative rotation, and a calibration that compensates against measured coil current. Both use signals the kit already carries, so the fix costs no new hardware, and both are rig tests rather than arguments.

Time

One hardware counter clocks the kit, and every sample is stamped when it is converted. Satellite time labels the epoch and never steers the clock, so jamming degrades position without also degrading timing. Timestamp uncertainty is under 100 µs against a requirement of about 1 ms. The ring: one machined billet with the fins cut into it

The ring carries the four fins, the fourteen magnets and the outer bearing races, and it is the only part of the kit that does not turn with the shell. It is machined as a single piece, because the cant angle of the fins is the guidance force and an assembly stack-up would spend it.

Why one piece rather than an assembly

Under launch the fins see about 85 N of transient aerodynamic load, and the ring has to carry its own setback without letting the bearing seats move. Two bearings, one retention collar, and a load path that bypasses them

The bearings are the only mechanical connection between the ring and the shell, and they are also where the friction budget lives. So the launch load is routed around them, and the collar that does it has been sized and checked.

The number that forced this arrangement

A ring assembly under 15,000 g of axial acceleration sees a setback load. On the assumed half-kilogram ring that was 74 kN; on the measured rotating assembly it is 276 kN, and every additional 0.1 kg of ring costs another 14.7 kN of retention.

Thin-section instrument bearings of the size this assembly needs carry static ratings in the single-digit kilonewtons, which is an order of magnitude short. Buying a bearing that could take the load would make it larger, heavier and higher-friction, breaking both the mass budget and the torque balance. So the requirement is not a stronger bearing but a load path that skips the bearings entirely: a seat the ring bears against under acceleration and releases from as the load decays.

The flight computer is two cores, and one of them does only one thing

The control loop runs a thousand times a second. The guidance law integrates the rest of the trajectory to impact about three hundred times per flight. Those are different jobs with different deadlines, so they run on different cores of the same device.

Software architecture

Everything that flies is written once, in portable C, behind fixed interfaces. The same source tree builds the flight image, a test binary for the rig, and a native library that the Monte Carlo campaign links against, so the campaign runs the code the flight image is built from.

A continuous-integration gate re-runs the bench's golden vectors on every change, so a bench result and a flight result cannot drift apart quietly. Precision is fixed per block — single precision in the loop, double in navigation and guidance — because the reference implementation's fixed-point arithmetic showed what quantisation can cost.

The fuze: three modes, two independent environments, one veto of its own

The kit replaces the fuze, so it has to be a better fuze and not only a guidance module. It offers three modes rather than the fielded pair, it arms on the launch environment itself, and it can refuse to function if the round is going to land far from where it was aimed.

The safety chain

The rotor that interrupts the explosive train is out of line in storage, in handling, in the bore and under ramming. It is driven into line mechanically by the launch environment, and it needs all three of: 15,000 g of setback, 176–304 Hz of spin, and an electrical arm command from the electronics.

The ordering is the safety argument. Because the rotor cannot complete its travel while setback is sustained, no in-bore function is possible by construction rather than by a timer. Dead electronics give a dud and never a premature burst, and a misfire is safe with no abort logic to get wrong.

The predicted-miss veto

The kit predicts where it will land and holds the target coordinates. If the predicted impact is still more than 150 m from the target at the point where guidance can no longer change the outcome, the fuze latches an inhibit and withholds the arm command, and the round becomes a dud instead of a miss. The decision time is not a clock: it is the moment the residual stops shrinking, which is the latest point our authority allows and the point at which our prediction error is smallest. Once latched it is irrevocable.

The mechanism adds nothing to the hardware, because it withholds a condition the arming chain already required. The cost is real and it is stated: a prediction error can dud a round that would have landed acceptably, and the dud-rate target now carries that deliberate contribution.

The guidance law: predict where it lands, ask for a direction that fixes it

Six times a second the kit takes its navigation state, integrates the rest of the flight forward to impact, compares that impact point with the target, and commands a ring angle. That is the whole law. Everything difficult about it is inside the word "integrates".

One cycle, in order

  1. Take the navigation state: position, velocity, attitude and time.
  2. Propagate it to impact under the onboard trajectory model, including the drag and the lift of the fins that are pushing the whole time.
  3. Compare the predicted impact point with the loaded target coordinates.
  4. Compute the ring angle that drives that miss to zero, using the authority actually available at this instant rather than an average.
  5. Send a commanded azimuth to the outer position loop. Nothing else: no force, no gain, no deflection.

Why this law rather than a pre-planned path

A path followed open-loop trusts the model that produced it. This law closes on measured state instead, so muzzle-velocity spread, meteorological error and launch perturbation are observed and corrected rather than assumed away. It also spends authority in proportion to the miss it can actually remove, which matters because the authority margin is 1.11 to 1 rather than comfortable.

How to aim a force that cannot be switched off

The fins push from launch to impact, the magnitude cannot be commanded, and the ring can only be turned at 31 rad/s against a real torque. The design answer is to stop treating the force as something to switch and treat it as something to schedule: hold the aim where it helps for part of each cycle, and park it broadside for the rest.

Why not simply slew to wherever the force is wanted

The ring rate is limited to about 31 rad/s and turning it against the aerodynamic torque takes time and current. A law that tried to point the force correctly on every cycle would spend the cycle slewing and never hold an aim. Duty-cycling inverts the problem: it puts the ring where it can stay, and buys the average it needs from the fraction of the cycle it holds.

The authority gate, and the failure it exists to prevent

Guidance authority is a window rather than a constant. Between about 35 s and 55 s there is not enough air pressure to steer with, and the design's answer is to compute that condition in flight and stop commanding, instead of assuming the window is continuous and losing the ring.

The failure this replaces

On hardware, with fixed saturation limits, the reference work lost control between 30 s and 50 s of flight. Near apogee the aerodynamic torque is too small to slow the ring, the velocity loop cannot respond in the direction it needs, and the ring overshoots: in the recorded case to about 5.5 rad against a 2.4 to 3.9 rad command set, with full rotation and non-linear instability as the escalation path.

The fix validated on hardware is what we implement — an observer that estimates the disturbance, plus current and rate limits scheduled from state rather than fixed. The gate is the same principle applied at the guidance layer instead of the control layer, and it is the piece the reference work left as future work.

The observer, and the limits that move

The controller cannot measure the two torques that dominate its life: the aerodynamic torque on the ring and the friction in its bearings. So it estimates them, and then uses that estimate to decide how much control authority it is allowed to spend.

The disturbance observer

estimate = filter( plant⁻¹ · measured output − commanded input ) filter = 1 / (0.5 s + 1) corner about 0.32 Hz, rise about 1.1 s

The filter is deliberately slow, and that is a decision rather than a limitation. The two disturbances it estimates have no fast content: the sharpest real event in the flight is the apogee collapse and the return of the descent torque, which develops over tens of seconds. A slow estimate also keeps the hard limit below it stable, which matters because a limit driven by a noisy estimate is a plausible route back into the limit cycle the observer exists to prevent.

The consequence is stated rather than hidden: the scheduled limit is a filtered estimate and not a measurement, so the factor of two in the current limit is carried as a bound to be justified. It is also the knob to turn if the rig shows the limit lagging the collapse.

Why every limit has to move

With fixed limits the reference hardware lost control between 30 s and 50 s of flight, overshooting to about 5.5 rad against a 2.4 to 3.9 rad command and escalating towards full rotation. The aerodynamic torque there is a fifth of a newton-metre and the friction is 0.10 N·m, so the loop is working with a 1.7 to 1 margin, the tightest in the design. There is no flight regime in which a fixed limit is safe, so the design removes the possibility rather than tuning around it.

The two hardest items still owed

The aerodynamic coefficient set, because every accuracy number depends on it and no amount of control design substitutes for it. And the friction budget at flight speed, because if the bearings cannot deliver a flat, load-dominated characteristic, the control-pair sizing window closes and the architecture has to be re-examined rather than re-tuned.

The satellite-denied position, in one sentence

Anti-jam reception with inertial coasting is the primary answer, riding the same spin-axis aperture the design already carries; the seeker is the secondary. That is the fielded class's own answer, it needs no designator and no change to how the battery fights, and it keeps the kit inside the cost lane.

The decisions we want challenged

These are the open questions where a review would change what we do next rather than what we write down. Each is a real trade with a cost on both sides, and each one has an owner in the record.

Physics and architecture

  1. Cant angle. Raising it buys guidance force and costs dissipated power one for one, and it also costs range through drag. Three requirements claim the same knob and none of them owns it. Which should govern the choice?
  2. Ring diameter and the machine's flux path. A smaller ring would cut the aerodynamic step and give the fins more exposed span, but the machine stops fitting below about 96 mm. A pancake axial-flux machine might fit where a radial one does not. Open that trade now, and link it to the range requirement?
  3. Mass. The model is roughly twice the budget as drawn. Do we take the class of kit and state the real net effect on the platform, or hold the number and pay for it in material and lightening — or both?
  4. Preload. One value has to satisfy air-gap stiffness, the friction gate and a thermal feedback path. Specify it conservatively and accept friction, or push for the flat characteristic and accept stiffness?

Judgement calls that are yours, not ours

  1. The predicted-miss radius. We adopted the class figure of 150 m, and the failure direction is a dud. That is a doctrinal question as much as a technical one: an unguided round is a legitimate ballistic effect, and the decision about where a 43 kg high-explosive round lands when guidance has failed may belong to the user's doctrine rather than to us.
  2. The exposed length. Accepting about 11 mm more exposure than the fielded benchmark is what makes the force requirement close. Is a slightly longer kit acceptable against the drop-in claim, or should the force requirement be re-derived downwards?
  3. The seeker posture. Keeping it out of the baseline protects the cost story and the one-moving-part claim, and it means we carry no satellite-denied capability beyond jamming resistance and inertial coasting. Confirm that is how it should be presented.

Inputs we need a route to

  1. The aerodynamic coefficient set. Published body and canard data with computation to fill gaps is our assumption. If a better route is available to us, it changes the schedule more than any other single item.
  2. The customer's drawing and firing tables. Everything platform-side is currently a declared envelope built from public data; the re-baseline is a phase rather than a task, and it moves the stability result first.
  3. The rig's scope. Flight-representative speed, the real generation path and the ring joint are what make it worth building. A scaled-down version would repeat the reference's mistake, so this is the one place where we would rather be told no than be cut down.

What happens next

Ruling the findings on page 33, then acquiring or computing the coefficient set, then the three verification layers in order: simulation, hardware in the loop, and the rig if the entry is shortlisted. The register, the CAD workspace and this briefing are all generated or traced from the same numbers, so a decision in one place propagates rather than being re-typed.
