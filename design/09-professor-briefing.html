DRISHTI-PGK: a precision guidance kit and multi-mode fuze for the 155 mm shell Technical briefing — design basis, hardware, flight behaviour and control architecture

The kit replaces the fuze and the forward ogive of a standard 155 mm high-explosive shell. Four fixed fins on a de-spun ring steer the round; the machine that turns the ring also generates the kit's electrical power. There are no actuators, no linkage and no flight battery. The shell, the charge system and the gun are untouched.

This briefing covers the physics the design rests on, every part and what it does, the flight from launch to impact, the guidance and control stack, and the state of the evidence: what the CAD has verified, what is still open, and where the design rests on a published figure rather than our own measurement.

Every quantity on these pages traces to a row in the design criteria register. Page references for each figure and table are given in the captions; the register is the authority if anything here disagrees with it.

The background it gives

“The YIL (Yantra India Limited) possesses extensive expertise in manufacturing the mechanical hardware of 155 mm artillery shells and seeks innovative solutions to transform conventional shells into precision-guided munitions through the integration of an advanced guidance and fuze system.”

The statement is therefore an integration task with a named supplier: the mechanical hardware exists, the guidance and the fuze are what it is asking for.

What it asks to be integrated

  • A canard actuation assembly for in-flight trajectory correction.
  • A guidance, navigation and control system capable of improving terminal accuracy.
  • A multi-mode electronic fuze supporting proximity mode, time mode and impact mode.

  • Power management suitable for high-g launch and flight conditions.
  • A communication and programming interface for fuze configuration prior to firing.

What we read as the hard part

It is not the accuracy figure. Two of the fielded systems on page 7 already claim 20 m and better, so 30 m is a target to be met rather than a record to be beaten. The hard part is the sentence that asks for survival and low cost at the same time: a machine that makes useful side force while turning at 1,912 rad/s, held inside a friction gate whose margin is 0.157 N·m against a build tolerance that has to hold in production.

Everything else follows from that. The thermal path exists because the machine's losses have nowhere to go; the authority gate exists because authority is small at the top of the trajectory and cannot be conjured; the power architecture exists because the machine is the only generator on board.

What we are not claiming Not a new guidance law, not a new airframe, not a new aerodynamics result. The novelty claim is narrow and it is on page 45: a gate that computes what a correction is worth before commanding it, and a machine that is simultaneously the actuator and the generator. The rest we took from the fielded record on purpose, and page 10 says so item by item.

Our design · architecture

Architecture: two bodies in relative rotation, and one interface between them

The kit screws into the existing fuze well, so the shell, the charge system and the gun stay as they are. Inside the kit there are only two bodies: everything that turns with the shell, and the ring, which does not. The bearing pair carries the structural load between them; the electric machine carries the torque.

Why this architecture

  • Steering comes from the cant of the fins, not from moving surfaces, so aiming the force means turning one ring. Two-dimensional steering needs one actuation axis.
  • The same machine that turns the ring generates the kit's power, so there is no flight battery to charge, store or qualify for shelf life.
  • The fins are permanently exposed. There is no deployment mechanism and no stored spring, so nothing can deploy in the bore — the in-bore question becomes a clearance calculation rather than a mechanism.
  • The fuze keeps its own processor, board and power path. The guidance computer can send a bounded one-way time correction and a veto, and nothing else.

Why the force cannot be commanded

Reading the arrangement

Figure 8(b) on the previous page shows the arrangement as seen down the axis. Torque, and why the bus voltage is a free choice

T = 3 · p · ψPM · I_rms / √2 at fixed slot area: T = p · k_w · Φpole · fill · A_slot · J / √2

The turns count cancels. At a fixed slot area the torque depends on pole count, flux per pole, slot area and current density, and not on how the winding is split between volts and amps. Copper loss is therefore the same at any bus voltage, and the voltage can be chosen on other grounds. A failure mode with its own name

Gyroscopic stability is not the whole story. A muzzle stability factor does not answer it, because it is an angular-motion effect rather than a gyroscopic margin.

The design has already taken the fix that the same literature recommends: reduce the span and get the force from cant rather than from area, which raises the natural frequency clear of the disturbance. The fuze: three modes, two independent environments, one veto of its own

The kit replaces the fuze, so it has to be a better fuze and not only a guidance module. It offers three modes rather than the fielded pair, it arms on the launch environment itself, and it can refuse to function if the round is going to land far from where it was aimed.

The safety chain

The rotor that interrupts the explosive train is out of line in storage, in handling, in the bore and under ramming. It is driven into line mechanically by the launch environment, and it needs all three of: 15,000 g of setback, 176–304 Hz of spin, and an electrical arm command from the electronics.

The ordering is the safety argument. Because the rotor cannot complete its travel while setback is sustained, no in-bore function is possible by construction rather than by a timer. Dead electronics give a dud and never a premature burst, and a misfire is safe with no abort logic to get wrong.

The predicted-miss veto

The kit predicts where it will land and holds the target coordinates. If the predicted impact is still more than 150 m from the target at the point where guidance can no longer change the outcome, the fuze latches an inhibit and withholds the arm command, and the round becomes a dud instead of a miss. The decision time is not a clock: it is the moment the residual stops shrinking, which is the latest point our authority allows and the point at which our prediction error is smallest. Once latched it is irrevocable.

The mechanism adds nothing to the hardware, because it withholds a condition the arming chain already required. The cost is real and it is stated: a prediction error can dud a round that would have landed acceptably, and the dud-rate target now carries that deliberate contribution.

The guidance law: predict where it lands, ask for a direction that fixes it

Six times a second the kit takes its navigation state, integrates the rest of the flight forward to impact, compares that impact point with the target, and commands a ring angle. That is the whole law. Everything difficult about it is inside the word "integrates".

One cycle, in order

  1. Take the navigation state: position, velocity, attitude and time.
  2. Propagate it to impact under the onboard trajectory model, including the drag and the lift of the fins that are pushing the whole time.
  3. Compare the predicted impact point with the loaded target coordinates.
  4. Compute the ring angle that drives that miss to zero, using the authority actually available at this instant rather than an average.
  5. Send a commanded azimuth to the outer position loop. Nothing else: no force, no gain, no deflection.

Why this law rather than a pre-planned path

A path followed open-loop trusts the model that produced it. This law closes on measured state instead, so muzzle-velocity spread, meteorological error and launch perturbation are observed and corrected rather than assumed away. It also spends authority in proportion to the miss it can actually remove, which matters because the authority margin is 1.11 to 1 rather than comfortable.

How to aim a force that cannot be switched off

The fins push from launch to impact, the magnitude cannot be commanded, and the ring can only be turned at 31 rad/s against a real torque. The design answer is to stop treating the force as something to switch and treat it as something to schedule: hold the aim where it helps for part of each cycle, and park it broadside for the rest.

Why not simply slew to wherever the force is wanted

The ring rate is limited to about 31 rad/s and turning it against the aerodynamic torque takes time and current. A law that tried to point the force correctly on every cycle would spend the cycle slewing and never hold an aim. Duty-cycling inverts the problem: it puts the ring where it can stay, and buys the average it needs from the fraction of the cycle it holds.

Guidance authority is a window rather than a constant. Between about 35 s and 55 s there is not enough air pressure to steer with, and the design's answer is to compute that condition in flight and stop commanding, instead of assuming the window is continuous and losing the ring.

The failure this replaces

On hardware, with fixed saturation limits, the reference work lost control between 30 s and 50 s of flight. Near apogee the aerodynamic torque is too small to slow the ring, the velocity loop cannot respond in the direction it needs, and the ring overshoots: in the recorded case to about 5.5 rad against a 2.4 to 3.9 rad command set, with full rotation and non-linear instability as the escalation path.

The fix validated on hardware is what we implement — an observer that estimates the disturbance, plus current and rate limits scheduled from state rather than fixed. The gate is the same principle applied at the guidance layer instead of the control layer, and it is the piece the reference work left as future work.

The observer, and the limits that move

The controller cannot measure the two torques that dominate its life: the aerodynamic torque on the ring and the friction in its bearings. So it estimates them, and then uses that estimate to decide how much control authority it is allowed to spend.

The disturbance observer

estimate = filter( plant⁻¹ · measured output − commanded input ) filter = 1 / (0.5 s + 1) corner about 0.32 Hz, rise about 1.1 s

The filter is deliberately slow, and that is a decision rather than a limitation. The two disturbances it estimates have no fast content: the sharpest real event in the flight is the apogee collapse and the return of the descent torque, which develops over tens of seconds. A slow estimate also keeps the hard limit below it stable, which matters because a limit driven by a noisy estimate is a plausible route back into the limit cycle the observer exists to prevent.

The consequence is stated rather than hidden: the scheduled limit is a filtered estimate and not a measurement, so the factor of two in the current limit is carried as a bound to be justified. It is also the knob to turn if the rig shows the limit lagging the collapse.

Why every limit has to move

With fixed limits the reference hardware lost control between 30 s and 50 s of flight, overshooting to about 5.5 rad against a 2.4 to 3.9 rad command and escalating towards full rotation. The aerodynamic torque there is a fifth of a newton-metre and the friction is 0.10 N·m, so the loop is working with a 1.7 to 1 margin, the tightest in the design. There is no flight regime in which a fixed limit is safe, so the design removes the possibility rather than tuning around it.

The decisions we want challenged

These are the open questions where a review would change what we do next rather than what we write down. Each is a real trade with a cost on both sides, and each one has an owner in the record.

Physics and architecture

  1. Cant angle. Raising it buys guidance force and costs dissipated power one for one, and it also costs range through drag. Three requirements claim the same knob and none of them owns it. Which should govern the choice?
  2. Ring diameter and the machine's flux path. A smaller ring would cut the aerodynamic step and give the fins more exposed span, but the machine stops fitting below about 96 mm. A pancake axial-flux machine might fit where a radial one does not. Open that trade now, and link it to the range requirement?
  3. Mass. The model is roughly twice the budget as drawn. Do we take the class of kit and state the real net effect on the platform, or hold the number and pay for it in material and lightening — or both?
  4. Preload. One value has to satisfy air-gap stiffness, the friction gate and a thermal feedback path. Specify it conservatively and accept friction, or push for the flat characteristic and accept stiffness?

Judgement calls that are yours, not ours

  1. The predicted-miss radius. We adopted the class figure of 150 m, and the failure direction is a dud. That is a doctrinal question as much as a technical one: an unguided round is a legitimate ballistic effect, and the decision about where a 43 kg high-explosive round lands when guidance has failed may belong to the user's doctrine rather than to us.
  2. The exposed length. Accepting about 11 mm more exposure than the fielded benchmark is what makes the force requirement close. Is a slightly longer kit acceptable against the drop-in claim, or should the force requirement be re-derived downwards?
  3. The seeker posture. Keeping it out of the baseline protects the cost story and the one-moving-part claim, and it means we carry no satellite-denied capability beyond jamming resistance and inertial coasting. Confirm that is how it should be presented.

Inputs we need a route to

  1. The aerodynamic coefficient set. Published body and canard data with computation to fill gaps is our assumption. If a better route is available to us, it changes the schedule more than any other single item.
  2. The customer's drawing and firing tables. Everything platform-side is currently a declared envelope built from public data; the re-baseline is a phase rather than a task, and it moves the stability result first.
  3. The rig's scope. Flight-representative speed, the real generation path and the ring joint are what make it worth building. A scaled-down version would repeat the reference's mistake, so this is the one place where we would rather be told no than be cut down.

What happens next

Ruling the findings on page 43, then acquiring or computing the coefficient set, then the three verification layers in order: simulation, hardware in the loop, and the rig if the entry is shortlisted. The register, the CAD workspace and this briefing are all generated or traced from the same numbers, so a decision in one place propagates rather than being re-typed.
