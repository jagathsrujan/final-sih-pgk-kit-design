Proposed Solution (Describe your Idea / Solution / Prototype)
‹Team Name›  ·  DRISHTI-PGK — a guidance kit and smart fuze for 155 mm shells

A kit that bolts onto the shell you already make

  • The problem, in one line. Most 155 mm shells fly unguided. The farther they fly, the wider they scatter — up to about 267 m at full range[1]. Hitting one small target then means firing many rounds: more ammunition, more time, more risk to everything nearby.
  • Our idea. DRISHTI-PGK replaces only the fuze and the nose of the shell. The shell body, the charge and the gun stay exactly as they are.
  • How it steers. Four fixed canards sit on a ring that turns freely around the shell, held by two bearings. Two opposite canards push the shell sideways; the other two only twist the ring. Turning the ring points that push in any direction — full steering, with no servo motors and no gears[14][9].
  • One machine, two jobs. An alternator in the ring makes electricity in flight. When the computer draws current from it, the ring turns instead. The same machine is both motor and generator.
  • Smart fuze on top. Three modes — proximity, time and impact — programmed on the gun line with the setter the army already uses[18].
  • What is actually new here. This layout matches the fielded US M1156 kit — the very system this problem statement asks India to build at home[2]. We are not reinventing it. We size it for our shells, solve its heat problem, and make it work when GPS is jammed.
  • Being honest about the cost. Holding the ring against the spinning shell makes heat: 2.92 kW at peak, about 121 kJ over a flight. We carry it out through the fuze well on purpose. Flight power is self-generated; a small reserve battery covers the moments before launch and the safe state.
Drawn after the fixed-canard 2-D guidance patent [14] and the canard-guided fuze study [9].

Technical Approach
‹Team Name›  ·  DRISHTI-PGK

How it finds the target, and how it holds course

  • Finding the target. The kit keeps predicting where the shell will land, compares that with the target loaded before firing, and turns the ring to close the gap — recomputed all the way down[7].
  • Knowing which way is which. The canard ring is already free of the shell's spin, so the sensors ride on it. They see the shell's slow wobble instead of a 15,000–20,000 rpm blur. That turns the hardest sensing problem in a spinning shell into an ordinary one.
  • The senses. GPS for position, an inertial unit for motion, a barometer for height — and a magnetic pickoff that tells the computer exactly where the ring is pointing.
  • The steering loop. Two nested controllers — fast inside, slow outside — running 1,000 times a second, with limits that adjust themselves as the air thins near apogee, so control never suddenly collapses[6].
  • If GPS is jammed. The kit coasts on its inertial sensors and keeps correcting. The fielded kit is getting anti-jam protection right now; by the time this flies, it will be expected, not extra.
  • Safety comes first. The fuze arms only after launch — it needs both the kick of firing and the spin. And if the kit predicts the round will land more than 150 m from the target, it refuses to detonate: the same 150 m rule the fielded kit uses[2].
Drawn after the guidance-kit controller of [6] and the impact-point-prediction law of [7].

Feasibility and Viability
‹Team Name›  ·  DRISHTI-PGK

The numbers close — and the concept has already run on hardware

Where the errors add up (1σ each):

Error sourceSize
Knowing where we are (GPS/INS)5 m
Guidance and steering17 m
Wind and muzzle-speed leftovers5 m
Air-data and model gaps8 m
Fuze and detonation scatter3 m
Combined → expected miss circle (CEP)≈ 23.9 m

That is inside the 30 m requirement — with the largest single term being "where will it land" (13 m of the 17 m), which is exactly where our design effort goes.

  • Enough steering force? Yes. Even at its weakest the canard pair still pushes with 18.1 N — 450 m of correction against the 407 m we need. A 1.11:1 margin, stated as a margin, not as comfort.
  • Has anything like this run before? Yes. Research groups have built and tested this exact architecture in the 155 mm class — including the weak-force region near the top of the trajectory — on a motor-generator bench like the one we plan[6][10][11].

The honest list of problems, and what we do about each:

  • Air-data gaps — our biggest unknown. We fill them with CFD and published data, then carry the uncertainty into the statistics instead of assuming it away.
  • Jamming. Anti-jam GPS plus inertial coasting.
  • Heat from the ring. Carried out through a purpose-built fuze-well joint.
  • Launch shock. Loads routed around the bearings; electronics potted; the ring machined from one piece.
  • The safety veto could dud a good round. So the decision is made as late as the steering allows.

How we prove it: thousands of Monte Carlo runs — the CEP is measured, not claimed — then a bench rig, then hardware in the loop. Built only if we are shortlisted; planned now.

Built to the standards the field expects: MIL-STD-1316 · MIL-STD-331C · MIL-STD-810F · MIL-STD-461 · AOP-22 · STANAG 4369/AOP-39 · STANAG 4439 · STANAG 9216/MIL-STD-333B.

Impact and Benefits
‹Team Name›  ·  DRISHTI-PGK

What it buys — for the gunner, the buyer, and Indian industry

  • For the gunner. First-round effect on point targets — 30 m or better, every range. No adjustment fire, shorter engagements, less time spent exposed.
  • For the commander. Far less collateral damage — an operational, political and legal benefit at once.
  • For the buyer. About ₹6.5 lakh per kit (≈ US$7,800) at 5,000 a year — against the ≈ US$13,541 the competing kit is listed at[3]. No new gun, no new shell, no new supply chain: the same organisation that buys the shell buys the kit.
  • For Indian industry. Made on YIL's own strengths — precision machining, wound electrical machines, moulded fuze parts. The few unusual items (electronics, magnets, thermal joint) are named, not hidden. The magnets even use less heavy rare earth, not more.
  • The ammunition math. The fielded programme's own record: 301 shells become 77 for the same effect, and 959 charges become 208[1]. Shells not fired are trucks not driven and depots not built.
  • Room to grow. Anti-jam GPS, a better seeker or another fuze can be swapped in later without redesigning the kit. The same idea scales to other calibres and to spin-stabilised rockets.

The one-sentence pitch:

A cheap kit that turns the shells India already makes into precision rounds — at roughly a third of the going price, on machines we already own.

What we will not claim. The volume (5,000 a year) is a plan, not a contract. The competing kit has 100,000+ units in the field. We name the gap — our case is that the capability must exist here first.
