Proposed Solution

The kit replaces the fuze, not the shell

  • It screws into the standard fuze well. The shell body, the charge system, the driving band and the gun interface never change.
  • Of the kit, 124.7 mm sits inside the well and 95.25 mm stands proud of it, on the same 2″-12 thread an in-service fuze uses.
  • Four small canards sit on a ring that is free to turn. Two of them steer. Two of them hold the ring against the shell’s spin, and holding it is what makes the machine generate.
  • Nothing moves except that ring — no servos, no gearboxes, no linkages. The ring’s angle is the only thing we command.
  • The canards stay out. Nothing deploys, so nothing can deploy at the wrong moment, and a 151 mm span keeps them clear of the bore.
  • Flight power comes from the same machine, so there is no flight battery. A reserve cell covers the safe state on the gun line; a capacitor bank covers the surges.
  • The fuze does all three modes: proximity from 2 to 15 m, time from 0.1 to 199.9 s, and impact.
  • It cannot arm in the bore. Two launch conditions must happen first — setback and spin — before the electronics get any say.
  • Target cost ₹6.5 lakh a unit at 5 000 a year, which is where we want to sit against an imported kit.
Most of this architecture is not ours
  • De-spun ring, fixed canards, no actuators, no flight battery — that is the published specification of the M1156 kit that the problem statement asks us to indigenise.
  • What we are adding is the sizing, the thermal path and the sensing. We would rather say that first than have a panel say it for us.

Technical Approach

How it aims, and how it holds the ring

Finding the target

  • The round works out where it is going to land, every cycle, and turns the ring to cancel the miss.
  • Impact-point guidance for this kind of airframe is published work. What we added is the gate — the law only commands when there is enough air pressure left to actually steer with.
  • The IMU rides the ring, so the shell’s spin comes off mechanically instead of being filtered out. A cheap ±2 000 °/s part is enough.
  • The GNSS antenna sits on the spin axis and is symmetric about it, so the spin does not modulate the signal at all.

Holding the ring

  • A 1 kHz loop inside, the guidance law outside it at about 6 Hz.
  • An observer estimates the aerodynamic torque we cannot measure and feeds it forward, so the loop is not chasing it.
  • Every limit moves with the flight. Fixed limits are what lost control in the reference work; we schedule against what the machine can actually deliver at that moment.
  • If the dump runs hot we take authority away gradually rather than switching anything off.

The hardware

  • A frameless outrunner, 14 poles and 12 slots, about 0.9 kg. The ring is its rotor.
  • One bus, held at 180 V, low enough that the converter never has to change character mid-flight.
  • The boards stack as rings inside the well, around the detonation path, so they take no room from the nose.
  • Power reaches the ring by transformer and data by light. The fuze keeps its own processor and its own supply, so a fault on our side never reaches it.

Feasibility and Viability

It closes. Here is where it is tight

And the hardware is now drawn: the kit body is modelled against the fuze well from our own numbers — 124.70 mm intrusion, zero interference with the reference shell, and the seat annulus that carries the 121 kJ matches the register’s own area to 1.3 %.

Does the accuracy close?

  • Yes. The budget adds to 24 m against the 30 m we are claiming.
  • Navigation is the part we can check best: 3.7 m against the 5 m we allowed for it.
  • The biggest single line is prediction error, not hardware. That is why the aerodynamic data is the one dependency we are loud about.
  • We will publish how many rounds land outside 30 m, not only the middle of the group.

And where does it land if it cannot get there?

  • The kit knows where it is going to land, every cycle. If that prediction says it will land more than 150 m from the loaded target, it will not function.
  • That is the same call the fielded kit makes, and it is what lets a battery call fires near its own position.
  • The cost is real: a bad prediction can dud a round that would have landed acceptably — so we decide as late as the authority allows.

Where it is tight

  • Steering authority: we need 407 m of correction and we have 450. That is 1.11 to 1, and we would rather show you the margin than round it up.
  • Near the top of the flight the aerodynamic torque nearly vanishes while friction does not. Friction is what decides whether the control pair can exist at all.
  • The kit makes 2.92 kW at its peak and about 121 kJ over the flight, and all of it has to leave through the fuze threads as heat. A bare thread will not carry that.
  • 75 kN of setback cannot go through the flight bearings. It goes around them.
  • Stability holds across the envelope, and we have named the resonance mode the literature reports for this exact configuration rather than waiting to be asked about it.

What we cannot claim yet

  • Five things are still open: ring friction at real flight speed, the thermal joint, sensor coexistence, the ring-to-body link, and whether the capacitor bank survives launch. All five are bench items.
  • The rig is specified and will be built if we are shortlisted. Until then these stay open, and we say so.
  • The friction figure we inherited was measured about twenty times slower than flight. It is a test question, not a fact.
  • The aerodynamic data is the long pole. It can still move every number on this slide.
  • Our platform numbers come from public data on this class of shell. Once we have your drawing, stability is re-run first.
  • And building the model priced two more: mass (the body alone reaches the 1.4 kg limit in steel, so material and lightening are the levers) and the reserved centreline bore, which costs the machine about 7 % of its slot depth. Both go to the reconciliation pass.
  • One thing we would still like you to confirm: the 5 000-a-year volume that the cost target rests on. open

Impact and Benefits

What it buys, and what it costs

Cost and volume

  • ₹6.5 lakh a unit at 5 000 a year — about 40 % below what the fielded kit sells for.
  • That is a recurring price. Development and tooling are not in it, so it compares like for like with the imported article.
  • The volume is an assumption, not a forecast. We have written it down so you can correct it.
  • The kit’s cost sits mostly in the machine, the power electronics and the bearings. Make-or-buy has not been settled yet, and it is the biggest lever we have on this number.
  • The magnets are the one strategic-material item, so we spec them tightly: the coercivity is bought with the heavy rare earth placed only at the grain boundaries — the same temperature class with less of the critical element, not more.
Two things we are not claiming
  • The fielded programme builds far more of these a year than we have assumed, and it already fields anti-jam electronics. Our target is a price and a volume, not a claim to have matched it.
  • The 5 000-a-year figure is ours to justify and yours to correct.

On the gun line

  • Four guided rounds do the work of about 264 today, for a 90 % chance against a point target.
  • No adjustment fire. The battery shoots and moves.
  • Range retention is written down as a requirement — better than 90 % — so accuracy cannot be bought out of the shell’s reach.
  • Nothing new to buy: same shell, same charge, same gun, same setter on the gun line.
  • Collateral effect falls with the group size. We state the accuracy claim in a form you can check — at the aimpoint, with target-location error shown separately rather than buried in the number.

What India gets to build

  • The shell body and the rifling interface are untouched, so the round itself needs no re-qualification.
  • The kit is heavier than the fuze it replaces — about 0.7 to 0.9 kg more, with the nose about 7 mm further forward. That sits inside the round’s own spread, and we would rather tell you than have you find it.
  • The three modes line up with the Army’s own requirement for 155 mm electronic fuzes. The programmable time mode is the part the fielded kit does not offer.
  • No seeker in the baseline. The GPS-denied answer is anti-jam GNSS with inertial coasting, riding an antenna we already have.
  • It is a consumable added to a round you already buy, bought through the channel you already buy through — no new platform, no new training, no new supply chain.
  • Most of the work is what a shell factory already does: the ring and canards come off one machining setup, the stator core is pressed or stamped, the winding is conventional. The printed-copper and printed-iron parts we evaluated stay in development, and their cost is not in the price.
  • The complex low-volume parts come out of a digital cell: robot printing, the post-treatment chain that makes printed parts fit for gun launch, and automated electronics placement — so for those parts a design change is a file, not a new die. It also places components inside the structure while printing, and prints the conductors between them — removing the connectors and harness that fail first under launch acceleration. Printed structural supports for circuit boards have already been shown to survive gun-launch shock.
  • The sensing stack is modular, with redundancy targeted rather than blanket: a second inertial and magnetic channel and a second ring-angle pickoff cost about one per cent of the kit’s mass. Bearings, detonation path and GNSS stay single, and we say why.
